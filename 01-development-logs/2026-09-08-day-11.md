# Day 11 — Tuesday 8 September — Mini App: Authentication and Roles/Permissions

**Scheduling note:** Day 10 ran on this date after a one-day displacement, and Day 11 ran the same day to bring the week back onto its dated schedule. Both hours logged separately as *Technical Development / Upskilling*. Recorded here rather than absorbed silently, per framework §2.

## Built

Sanctum token authentication — `register`, `login`, `logout`, `me` — plus the two-role Policy layer and the Day 7 error envelope wired into the exception handler.

- **Four policies:** `SkillPolicy`, `DevelopmentPlanPolicy`, `AssessmentPolicy`, `WeeklyEntryPolicy`.
- **The integrity rule** implemented as a second condition on every write policy: `$user->isAdministrator() && $user->id !== $plan->user_id`. Nobody records, amends or closes an assessment or weekly entry on their own plan — administrator included.
- **Error envelope** `{error, code, message, details}` on every failure path: 401, 403, 404, 405, 422, 429.
- **Two thin authorisation-surface routes** (`GET /plans/{plan}`, `POST /plans/{plan}/assessments`) so the policies are provable over real HTTP today rather than only through the Gate. Day 12 completes them with Form Requests and API Resources.

**Verified:** 19 real HTTP checks as both roles, plus the full permission matrix evaluated through the Gate. 19 of 19 as designed. Evidence: `04-logs/evidence-logs/2026-09-08-day-11.md`.

## Learned

Authorisation answers **"is this your call?"**. It does not answer **"is this the right moment?"**. Baseline immutability after activation, contiguous week numbers, a final requiring a matching baseline — those are state-machine rules and belong to Day 13's service, not to a Policy. Putting them in a Policy would have been easy and would have made every 403 ambiguous: the user could not tell *you are not allowed* from *not yet*. Keeping the two apart is what makes a 403 actionable.

The related point: a weekly entry carries an `outcome_score`, so it is a **scoring action** even though it reads as progress tracking. Governing it more softly than an assessment would have left a hole straight through the integrity rule.

## Got wrong

1. **Carried Day 8's login failure signal forward without reading what it produced.** `ValidationException::withMessages()->status(401)` returns an envelope reading `code: "validation_failed"` alongside HTTP 401. The shape was right and the meaning was wrong — a client keying on `code`, which is the entire point of having one, would treat a rejected sign-in as a malformed form. Found by reading the actual response body in the first verification run, not by reviewing the code. Fixed with a dedicated `InvalidCredentialsException` → `code: "invalid_credentials"`.
2. **The first rate-limit evidence was worthless and nearly went into the log.** All seven attempts returned 429 because the earlier login checks had already consumed the IP-keyed quota. The behaviour was correct; the evidence showed nothing, because it never captured the transition from allowed to throttled. A screenshot of seven 429s would have looked like proof and proved nothing.
3. **`throttle:5,1` on login was wrong for this application and I had copied it forward unexamined.** It keys on IP and counts successful logins too. This is an internal tool whose users share one office IP, so five sign-ins on a Monday morning lock out the sixth colleague. Rekeyed on email + IP, and proved with a check that a *different* account from the same address can still sign in.
4. **Day 8's `register` accepted a `role` field from the request.** Carried forward, that would have put the whole authorisation model one payload field away from being bypassed — anyone could self-register as an administrator. Removed; registration always produces a `member`, and administrators are seeded. Tested explicitly (check 7).

That was wrong: a weekly entry carries `outcome_score`. Corrected before the verification run; had it shipped it would have left a route straight around the integrity rule.
